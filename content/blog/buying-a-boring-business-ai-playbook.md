You found a profitable, unglamorous business: a pest control company, a commercial cleaning outfit, an HVAC shop. The seller built it over 25 years, and much of how it runs lives in their head. You closed. Now what?

AI can be one of the best tools in an acquisition entrepreneur's kit, but only if it's used in the right order. This playbook covers what to look for before you close and what to do in your first 100 days.

## Why is AI such a big opportunity when you buy a boring business?

**Many boring businesses for sale run on manual processes and the seller's personal effort, so a new owner who adds automation can often tighten operations without adding headcount.** That operational upside is yours, because you're the one doing the work after close.

Common patterns in seller-run businesses:

- The seller answers the phone after hours.
- Quotes go out when the seller gets to them.
- Invoices are sent in batches at month-end.
- Nobody asks for reviews.
- The "reporting" is the seller's memory and the bank balance.

Each of these is a leak and a dependency on one person. Fixing them is a mix of process work and well-chosen [AI integration](/glossary/ai-integration).

There's a second reason AI matters in an acquisition: you're probably not going to work the way the seller did. Many sellers worked 60-hour weeks and did everything themselves. If you plan to manage rather than do every task personally, you need the work that lived in the seller's head and evenings to live somewhere else. Some of it goes to your team. Much of the repetitive part can go to automation.

## What should you look for during due diligence?

**Look for revenue leaks and owner dependence: missed calls, slow quotes, slow invoicing, aging receivables and tasks only the seller knows how to do.** These show you where automation can help after close.

| What to request | What it tells you | AI opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Phone system call logs (missed/unanswered) | How many leads go unanswered | AI receptionist, missed-call text-back |
| Quotes sent vs. quotes won | Whether follow-up exists | Automated quote follow-up |
| Days from job completion to invoice | Cash flow drag | Automatic invoicing |
| Accounts receivable aging | Collection discipline | Payment reminders |
| Google review count and recency | Online reputation and visibility | Review automation |
| List of tasks only the seller does | Owner dependence | SOPs, knowledge base, automation |

One caution: don't pay the seller for improvements you'll make. Value the business on what it does today. The automation upside is part of your return for doing the work.

Also check the tech stack itself. Note which software the business uses, who owns each account, whether data can be exported and whether contracts transfer. A business that runs on an old desktop program on the seller's personal laptop is a different project from one already on Jobber or QuickBooks Online. Neither is a deal-breaker, but both affect your first 100 days.

A few diligence questions to ask the seller directly:

- "What happens to calls that come in after 5 p.m.?"
- "Who follows up on quotes that don't close?"
- "If you were gone for two weeks, what would break first?"
- "Which reports do you look at every week, and where do they come from?"

The answers tell you more than the financials about where the hidden work is.

## What should you do in the first 30 days?

**In the first 30 days, stabilize and document: meet customers and staff, learn how work actually flows and capture the seller's knowledge before they leave.** Avoid changing customer-facing systems yet.

### Capture the seller's knowledge

This is the most valuable AI use in month one. During the transition period, record walkthroughs with the seller:

- How do you price a typical job?
- Which customers need special handling?
- Which suppliers do you call for what?
- What do you do when a crew calls in sick?

AI can transcribe these recordings and turn them into draft [SOPs](/glossary/sop) and a searchable [knowledge base](/glossary/knowledge-base). Your team can then ask "how do we price a commercial quarterly plan?" and get the seller's answer. Our post on [building an AI knowledge base for employee training](/blog/ai-knowledge-base-employee-training) explains how.

### Learn the numbers

Get read access to every system: accounting, field service software, phone, payroll, bank. Write down the baseline: monthly revenue, missed calls, quote close rate, days to invoice. You'll need it to measure everything that follows.

### Talk to your team

Tell staff what's coming and why. A line that works: "I want to get rid of the busywork so you can spend more time on the work that matters." Ask them which tasks they hate. Their answers will shape your list.

### Your 30-day checklist

- Every login, phone number, domain and software account transferred to you
- Recorded walkthroughs with the seller on pricing, key customers and suppliers
- Draft SOPs for the five most common jobs or tasks
- Baseline numbers written down
- One-on-one conversations with every employee
- A short list of the three biggest leaks you've found

## What should you automate in days 31 to 60?

**In days 31 to 60, automate the front office: answer every call, follow up on every quote and ask every happy customer for a review.** These changes win revenue quickly and are easy to measure against your baseline.

1. **Missed-call text-back** on the main line, so no lead goes unanswered.
2. **An [AI receptionist](/services/ai-receptionist)** for after-hours and overflow calls, which the seller probably answered personally.
3. **Quote follow-up** with a short sequence of check-ins.
4. **Review requests** after every completed job.
5. **Appointment reminders** to cut no-shows and reschedules.

Before you turn any of these on, introduce yourself to the customer base. A short letter or email from the new owner explaining that the same team is still there, and that you're investing in faster service, makes every later change feel like an upgrade rather than a disruption.

Write messages in the business's existing voice. Customers who've worked with the same company for ten years shouldn't suddenly get messages that feel corporate. For example:

> "Hi Tom, this is Green Valley Pest. Just a reminder your quarterly service is Thursday between 9 and 11. Reply if you need a different day. Thanks for being with us all these years!"

If you're texting customers, confirm you have their consent and register your texting number for A2P 10DLC. Keep TCPA rules in mind for anything that counts as marketing, and check current requirements before you launch.

## What should you automate in days 61 to 100?

**In days 61 to 100, automate the back office and reporting: invoicing, collections, data entry and a weekly dashboard that replaces the seller's gut feel.** This is where you free up your own time.

### Invoicing and collections

Connect your field service software to your accounting system so invoices go out when jobs close, with reminders on a schedule. Faster invoicing often means faster cash collection.

### Data entry

Look for the places where staff retype information: work orders into invoices, vendor bills into QuickBooks, job notes into the CRM. These are prime candidates for [back-office automation](/glossary/back-office-automation).

### Weekly reporting

Set up an automated Monday report with revenue, jobs completed, open quotes, receivables, missed calls and new reviews. Our guide to [AI dashboards and reporting](/blog/ai-dashboards-reporting-small-business) covers what to include.

This report also becomes the backbone of conversations with your lender or investors. Instead of scrambling for numbers when they ask, you'll have the same view every week, with trends against your day-one baseline.

### Decide on custom tools

By day 100, you'll know which spreadsheets and workarounds slow everyone down. Some will be worth replacing with [custom AI software](/services/custom-ai-software); others just need a better off-the-shelf tool.

## What mistakes do new owners make with AI?

**Common mistakes include changing too much too fast, automating before understanding the process and losing the personal touch with long-standing customers.**

- **Day-one overhauls.** Customers and staff are already nervous about new ownership. Don't add five new systems in week one.
- **Automating guesses.** If you don't yet know how pricing really works, AI will automate the wrong thing.
- **Cutting people first.** Your experienced staff hold relationships and knowledge you need. Use AI to remove busywork, not trust.
- **No baseline.** Without day-one numbers, you can't tell what worked.
- **Ignoring data access.** Make sure accounts, phone numbers, domains and software logins transfer to you at close.

### An illustrative example of the payoff

This is a hypothetical example. Assume the business invoices about $100,000 a month and currently sends invoices around 20 days after jobs close. If automatic invoicing cut that to 2 days, you'd pull cash forward by about 18 days, roughly $100,000 × 18 ÷ 30 = $60,000 in working capital collected sooner, assuming customers pay on the same terms. That's a one-time cash timing benefit, not new revenue, but it matters a lot when you're carrying acquisition debt.
