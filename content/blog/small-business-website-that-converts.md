Many small business websites look fine but book very few jobs. They get traffic from Google, ads, and the truck wrap, and then visitors leave without calling. The fix usually isn't a full redesign. It's a short list of practical changes, plus a follow-up system behind the form so the leads you do get actually turn into jobs.

Here's the checklist we use when reviewing a service business website.

## What makes a small business website actually book jobs?

**A website books jobs when it answers three questions in seconds (what you do, where you do it, and how to reach you) and then makes reaching you effortless.** Everything else is secondary.

Think about how a real customer arrives. A homeowner with a leaking water heater searches on their phone, taps your listing, and gives your site maybe five seconds. If they can't see "Water heater repair in [your town]" and a phone number they can tap, they hit the back button and call the next result.

So a converting site does a few unglamorous things very well:

- Loads fast on a phone
- States the service and service area in plain words at the top
- Puts a tap-to-call button and a short quote form within easy reach on every page
- Shows proof you're real and good at the work
- Responds to every inquiry immediately, even at 9 p.m.

Here's the full checklist in one view, then we'll go through each piece.

| Area | What "good" looks like | Quick test |
|---|---|---|
| Speed | Main content visible in about 2.5 seconds on mobile | Run Google PageSpeed Insights on your home page and top service page |
| Mobile | Readable text, thumb-sized buttons, no sideways scrolling | Open your site on your own phone with one hand |
| Click-to-call | Tappable phone number in the header and a sticky call button | Tap it. Does your phone app open with the number filled in? |
| Headline | Service + area + outcome above the fold | Could a stranger say what you do in five seconds? |
| Service pages | One page per core service, with pricing guidance and FAQs | Count your service pages vs. your actual services |
| Trust | Reviews, real photos, license/insurance, named people | Would you trust this company with your house keys? |
| Forms | 4–5 fields, works on mobile, clear next step | Fill it out yourself on your phone and time it |
| Follow-up | Instant text/email reply plus team alert and CRM entry | Submit a test lead after hours and see what happens |

## How fast does a small business website need to be?

**Fast enough that the main content shows up in about 2.5 seconds on a phone, which is Google's benchmark for a "good" Largest Contentful Paint.** Slower than that and you're paying for visitors who leave before they see your phone number.

To check, paste your URL into Google PageSpeed Insights and look at the mobile results, not desktop. Test your busiest service page too.

The usual problems on small business sites are boring and fixable:

1. **Huge images.** A 6 MB photo straight off a phone camera on the home page. Resize and compress images, and use modern formats like WebP.
2. **Heavy themes and page builders.** Some templates load a lot of code you never use. Sometimes a lighter theme is the real fix.
3. **Too many plugins and scripts.** Chat widgets, old tracking pixels, review sliders, popups. Remove anything you can't name a reason for.
4. **Slow hosting.** Bargain shared hosting can drag everything down. Better hosting is often a small monthly difference.

Google also measures two other page experience signals: responsiveness (Interaction to Next Paint) and visual stability (Cumulative Layout Shift). In plain terms: buttons should respond when tapped, and the page shouldn't jump around while it loads so a customer taps the wrong thing.

## Why does mobile design matter so much for local service businesses?

**Because many people looking for a local service are on their phone, often while standing in the problem: the flooded basement, the dead AC, the car that won't start.** If your site is hard to use with one thumb, you lose them.

Do this test right now: open your website on your phone, hold it with one hand, and try to call you, then try to request a quote. Watch for:

- Text you have to pinch to read
- Buttons too small or too close together
- Menus that hide the phone number
- Popups that cover the screen and are hard to close
- Forms where the keyboard covers the field you're typing in

A good mobile layout puts a **sticky bar at the bottom of the screen with "Call" and "Get a quote" buttons** so the next step is always one tap away, no matter how far someone scrolls.

## How should click-to-call and contact options be set up?

**Make your phone number a tappable link in the header of every page, add a sticky call button on mobile, and offer a text option for people who don't want to talk.** Then make sure someone, or something, actually answers.

A few details that matter more than they look:

- **Use a real tap-to-call link** (a `tel:` link), not a phone number baked into an image.
- **Show your hours honestly, and say what happens after hours.** "Call 24/7, we answer every call" only works if you do.
- **Add text as an option.** Many customers, especially younger homeowners and busy property managers, prefer to text. A "Text us" button gives them a lower-pressure way in.
- **Track calls from the website.** A tracking number on the site (swapped dynamically, so your main number stays consistent on your Google Business Profile) shows you which pages and campaigns make the phone ring.

Here's the hard truth: click-to-call only converts if the call gets answered. If your techs are on jobs and the office is on another line, those website calls go to voicemail. ## What should a good service page include?

**A good service page is built around one service and one buyer: it says what you do, where you do it, roughly what it costs, what the process looks like, and why to choose you, with a call button and form on the page.** One crowded "Services" page with a bulleted list can't do that.

If you're a plumber, "drain cleaning," "water heater replacement," "sewer line repair," and "emergency plumbing" should each have their own page. A cleaning company might split "move-out cleaning," "recurring house cleaning," and "office cleaning." Each one gets a different searcher with different questions.

A simple service page template:

1. **Headline:** "Water Heater Replacement in Springfield and Nearby Towns"
2. **One-paragraph answer:** what you do, how fast you can come out, and who it's for
3. **Call and quote buttons**
4. **What's included / how it works:** three to five steps, from first call to cleanup
5. **Pricing guidance:** a range, a starting price, or what drives the price. Even "most replacements fall between X and Y, and we give a firm quote before we start" builds confidence.
6. **Proof:** two or three reviews that mention this service, and real photos of this type of job
7. **FAQs:** the questions your office actually gets on the phone
8. **Service area:** the towns and neighborhoods you cover

## Which trust signals make visitors choose you?

**Proof that you're a real, local, licensed business that does good work: genuine reviews, real job photos, license and insurance details, and the faces and names of the people who show up.** Stock photos of smiling models in hard hats do the opposite.

The trust signals that pull their weight on a service business site:

- **Reviews with names and specifics,** pulled from your Google Business Profile, shown near the call button and on each service page
- **Before-and-after photos** from your own jobs, with the town named
- **License numbers, insurance, and bonding,** stated plainly
- **Warranties and guarantees** in plain language, with any conditions spelled out
- **Your team:** owner photo, a short story, technician names
- **Manufacturer certifications** you actually hold, and a clear service area

If your review count is thin, fix the source rather than the website. A simple automated review request after every completed job is one of the easiest wins there is; see [how to get more Google reviews automatically](/blog/get-more-google-reviews-automatically).

## How long should a contact or quote form be?

**Short: name, phone, service needed, ZIP code or address, and an optional message is enough for most service businesses.** Every additional required field gives someone another reason to give up.

Compare these two forms:

**Before:** First name, last name, email, phone, street address, city, state, ZIP, how did you hear about us, preferred contact method, preferred date, preferred time, project budget, detailed description (required).

**After:** Name, mobile number, what do you need help with (dropdown), ZIP code, anything else we should know (optional).

The "after" form gives you everything you need to respond. Budget, timing, and photos can come by text in the first conversation.

A few other form details that matter:

- **Say what happens next** right on the button or just under it: "We'll text you within 5 minutes during business hours."
- **Ask for a mobile number** and label it that way so you can text back.
- **Include a consent checkbox** if you plan to send texts. Texting customers falls under the TCPA, and business texting in the US generally requires A2P 10DLC registration through your texting provider. Keep consent language clear and get proper guidance if you're unsure.
- **Test it.** Broken forms are more common than you'd think, especially after a plugin update. Submit a test lead every month.

## What should happen after someone submits your form?

**The person should get an instant, personal-sounding reply, your team should be alerted, the lead should land in your CRM, and a backup reminder should fire if nobody follows up quickly.** This is the part many websites skip, and it's where a lot of paid-for leads die.

Speed matters because many people request quotes from more than one company. Whoever responds first and clearly often gets the job. That's the idea behind [speed to lead](/glossary/speed-to-lead).

Here's what that looks like as a before-and-after workflow:

**Before:** Form submission goes to an email inbox the owner checks at night. Callback happens the next day, if someone remembers. By then, the customer may have already booked someone else.

**After:**

1. Form is submitted at 7:42 p.m.
2. Within seconds, the customer gets a text: "Hi Maria, thanks for reaching out to Summit Plumbing about your water heater. We've got your request. Can you reply with a photo of the unit and its label? We'll call you first thing tomorrow, or reply URGENT if you have an active leak."
3. The lead is created in your CRM or field service software (Jobber, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan, HubSpot, whatever you use) with the source page attached.
4. The on-call tech or office manager gets an alert.
5. If the lead isn't marked "contacted" by 9 a.m., the owner gets a reminder.
6. If the customer doesn't book, a short, polite follow-up sequence goes out over the next few days.

### A quick way to test your own follow-up

Tonight, after business hours, fill out your own website form using a friend's phone number. Then note:

- How long until they hear anything?
- Does the reply sound like a person or a robot?
- Does the lead appear in your CRM, or only in someone's email?
- When does a human actually call?

The answers usually show you where the leaks are.
